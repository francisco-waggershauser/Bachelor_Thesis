\section{Limitations}\label{sec:limitations}

\paragraph{One family of value distributions.}
All measured results rest on a single model: lognormal values whose ranks are linked by a common
component (Section~\ref{sec:instances}). The proven results do not depend on it.
Corollary~\ref{cor:bundlesize} and Proposition~\ref{prop:identical} hold for every valuation of the
stated kind, and Proposition~\ref{prop:theta-limit} holds for every level of agreement. Every EFX
rate, every count and every survival curve, however, could look different under another
distribution, for instance with heavier tails or with agents who differ in how dispersed their
values are. \textcite[Conclusion]{celine2026} make the same point for average case analyses of
picking sequences in general. Other models with a common and an individual component exist
\parencite{dickerson2014,branzei2025}; we did not run them.

\paragraph{Paired comparisons.}
All curves over agreement, dispersion and mechanisms use the same random numbers
(Section~\ref{sec:design}). Differences between curves are therefore estimated precisely, but the
counts over cells in Chapters~\ref{ch:results} and~\ref{ch:random} are not independent
confirmations. They show how consistent a pattern is within our design, not how likely it is to
hold in another one.

\paragraph{Existence assumed in the open region.}
For four or more agents with at least four goods more than agents and less than full agreement, it
is unknown whether an EFX allocation always exists. In this region, which contains the reference
cell, we assume existence (Section~\ref{sec:existence}). If the assumption were false for some
instances, part of the failures we attribute to Round Robin would be failures of the problem
itself. For two and three agents, under full agreement and for few goods, existence is proven, and
all our conclusions about Round Robin hold there without the assumption.

\paragraph{Agreement describes a world, not an instance.}
The Spearman value describes the distribution from which instances are drawn. With twelve goods,
the rank correlation between two agents in a single instance can differ considerably from it
(Section~\ref{sec:instances}). Our recommendations about agreement therefore refer to groups of
people who agree to a certain degree on average, not to a measured correlation in one situation.

\paragraph{Design choices.}
The balanced random allocation is a generous model of dividing the goods somehow, because it fixes
the bundle sizes (Section~\ref{sec:random}); an unbalanced random allocation would fare worse, so
the advantage of Round Robin is if anything understated. Two counts in Chapter~\ref{ch:results}
and Section~\ref{sec:reversed} are exploratory, because we fixed their rules after having seen
individual cells. Finally, our design covers two to five agents and at most four goods per agent.
Statements about larger settings rely on the literature, not on our data.

\paragraph{Truthful picking.}
In our model, every agent picks their favourite remaining good. In practice, an agent who knows the
others' preferences can sometimes do better by picking strategically, so sequential allocation is
in general not strategyproof \parencite[Sec.~4]{bouveret2011}. Drafts used in practice face the
same problem \parencite[p.~1065]{budish2011}. Under identical preferences, sequential allocation is
strategyproof \parencite[Prop.~6]{bouveret2011}, so our results under full agreement are not
affected. For the other levels of agreement, our EFX rates describe sincere picking.

\section{Outlook}\label{sec:outlook}

Several of our measured patterns lack a proof or an explanation, and each of them is a natural
next step.

\paragraph{Limits without divisibility.}
Without divisibility, the EFX rate does not approach $1$ as dispersion vanishes
(Section~\ref{sec:dispersion-limit}). We conjecture that the limit lies strictly between $0$ and
$1$ below full agreement. A proof would have to describe the comparison of first-order terms that
decides EFX when an agent with $r$ goods compares their bundle with one of $r+1$ goods.

\paragraph{No guarantee for positive dispersion.}
We proved that the EFX rate stays below $1$ for every positive dispersion only for the smallest
case, two agents with four goods. Extending this to every cell would show that the limit theorem
cannot be strengthened to a threshold below which Round Robin is always EFX.

\paragraph{Unexplained patterns.}
Three measured patterns remain without explanation: under full agreement, more goods per agent
lower the EFX rate instead of raising it (Section~\ref{sec:goods-rate}); the break at $m=2n+1$
deepens with agreement (Section~\ref{sec:bundlesize}); and among the cells without divisibility,
the EFX rate is lowest for a remainder in the middle (Section~\ref{sec:divisibility-rate}). The
argument that Round Robin is EF1 (Proposition~\ref{prop:rrEF1}) compares, round by round, what an
agent picked with what another agent picked one round later; following these differences across
rounds is a candidate for explaining all three.

\paragraph{Other distributions and other sequences.}
A robustness check with other value distributions would test how far our recommendations carry
over. Beyond reversing the last round, other simple picking sequences such as reversing the order
in every round are used in practice \parencite[p.~1065]{budish2011}; \textcite[Conclusion]{celine2026}
propose to examine whether their worst-case comparisons of such sequences carry over to the
average case, and an analysis with respect to EFX along the lines of this thesis would be one way
to do so.

\paragraph{Existence.}
Our simulations cannot settle whether EFX allocations always exist for four or more agents
\parencite[Open Problem~2]{amanatidis2023survey}. They only show where Round Robin finds one and
where an existence proof would make the failures we report unambiguously failures of the
mechanism.
