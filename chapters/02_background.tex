\section{Setting and Notation}\label{sec:setting}
FUNCTION: Introduces agents, goods, additive valuations, allocations, picking sequences and
Round Robin, together with the number of full rounds and the remainder of goods. All later
chapters use this notation.

We consider a set of agents $N = \{1,\dots,n\}$ as well as a set of indivisible goods $M = \{g_1,\dots,g_m\}$. Each agent has an additive \textit{valuation function} $v_i(S)$, where $S \subseteq M$ and $v_i(S)=\sum_{g\in S} v_i(g)$, with $v_i(g) \geq 0$. The model (see Chapter~\ref{ch:model}) generates strictly positive, continuous values. 

An \textit{allocation} $A = (A_1,\dots,A_n)$ is a tuple of subsets of $M$ ($A_i \subseteq M$), so that every agent $i \in N$ gets a \textit{bundle} assigned. The bundles are pairwise disjoint ($A_i \cap A_j = \emptyset$ for $i \neq j$), and complete ($\bigcup_{i \in N} A_i = M$).

We define a \textit{picking sequence} following \textcite{bouveret2011} as a sequence $(\sigma_1,\dots,\sigma_m) \in N^m$. In turn $k$ agent $\sigma_k$ chooses the good with the highest $v_{\sigma_k}(g)$ from the remaining goods. Ties are broken by a fixed rule; under continuous valuations they occur with probability zero (Chapter~\ref{ch:model}).

The central picking sequence of this work, the Round Robin algorithm, has the sequence $1,2,\dots,n,1,2,\dots$, truncated after $m$ turns. We define one round as round $t$, which contains the turns $(t-1)n+1$ until $tn$. The last round can be incomplete. The good that agent $i$ takes in round $t$ is $i_t$. The bundle of agent $i$ is $A_i=\{i_1,\dots,i_{|A_i|}\}$.

Following \textcite{manurangsi2021}, we define two variables $r$ and $q$. Here, $r$ is the number of \textit{full rounds} defined as $r = \lfloor \frac{m}{n} \rfloor$ and $q = m - nr$ which is the remainder of goods in the last round. If $q=0$ implies that $m$ is divisible by $n$ and there is no incomplete round. Applied to the Round Robin algorithm, that means that the agents $1,\dots,q$ get $r+1$ goods and the other remaining agents $r$ goods.

\begin{example}
Let $n=3$, $m=7$, thus $r = \lfloor \frac{7}{3}\rfloor =2$ and $q = 1$: the sequence of Round Robin is 1231231. The bundle sizes for the agents are 3, 2, 2.
\end{example}


\section{Fairness Notions}\label{sec:notions}
FUNCTION: Defines envy freeness, EF1, EFX and its approximate version, the alpha-EFX ratio used
as the target quantity, each attributed to its source. The section shows how the notions relate
to each other.

\subsubsection{Envy-freeness (EF)}

The fair division problem domain had developed various fairness concepts throughout the time. The goal is to find a \textit{fair allocation} that can fulfil such concept. One of the most established fairness notions is \textit{envy-freeness} (EF). We denote to \textcite{amanatidis2023survey} which defines (EF) as $v_i(A_i)\geq v_i(A_j) \forall i,j$. That means, agent $i's$ bundle has more value for him that any other bundle. For indivisible goods, it turned out that EF is a very strict notion and rarely achievable. The simple example of two agents $n=2$ with only one good $M = \{ g\}$ shows that. There is no allocation, where the agents without the good is not envious towards the other agent.

\subsubsection{Envy-freeness up to one good (EF1)}
Consequently, different relaxations of the envy-free notion began to emerge. One of the most established relaxations is \textit{envy-freeness up to on good} (EF1). The concept of EF1 goes back to \textcite{lipton2004}, and got defined by \textcite{budish2011} as follows: An Allocation $A$ is EF1, if $\forall i,j$ with $A_j \neq \emptyset$ there $\exists$ $g \in A_j$ with $v_i(A_i) \geq v_i(A_j \backslash \{g\})$. \textbf{HIER NOCH MIT DEM MAX VALUE OF $g\in A_j$}. Therefore, agent $i$ \textbf{is} envious towards agent $j$ because from his point of view, the bundle $A_j$ has more value than his own. However, agent $i$ stops being envious towards the bundle of $j$ if the most expensive good from is bundle gets eliminated. Its existence follows from the envy-cycle elimination algorithm of Lipton et al. (2004, Theorem 2.1 and Lemma 2.2).

\subsubsection{Envy-freeness up to any good}
Envy-freeness up to one good (EF1) turned out to be quite easily computable, as it is also a relatively weak fairness concept. The next major refinement of the fairness concept is \textit{envy-freeness up to any good} (EFX). The origin of this notion is \textcite{caragiannis2019}. The Allocation $A$ is EFX if $v_i(A_i) \geq v_i(A_j\backslash \{g\})$ $\forall i,j$ and $\forall$ $g \in A_j$ with $v_i(g) >0$. \textbf{HIER NOCH DEN MIN VALUE WÄHLEN}. In this case, agent $i$ \textbf{is} still envious towards agents $j's$ bundle $A_j$ but stops to envy his bundle after the \textbf{cheapest} (or any) good gets eliminated. 

\subsubsection{Hierarchy}
The current state of research has EFX as one of the main scopes in fair division theory. All of those fairness notions are logically connected. The following applies $EF \Rightarrow EFX \Rightarrow EF1$ but not the other way around. Ist jedes Bündel höchstens ein Gut groß, ist jede Allokation EFX, denn nach dem Entfernen bleibt nichts übrig. Bei $m\le n$ gibt RR jedem höchstens ein Gut. Deshalb gilt ab hier $m>n$, und $m=n$ (house allocation) ist abgegrenzt.

\subsubsection{$\astar$-EFX and our Target Value}
Instead of focusing on exact EFX allocations, a growing line of work has taken a different approach by aiming to compute allocations that are approximately EFX, for different notions of approximation. The first such notion is in terms of
multiplicative approximations to the values obtained by the agents. \textcite{amanatidis2023survey} defines $\alpha$-EFX as $v_i(A_i) \geq \alpha \cdot v_i(A_j \backslash\{g\})$ for all positive $g \in A_j$.
Hence, we can rearrange the $\alpha$-EFX equation to $\alpha_{ij} = \frac{v_i(A_i)}{v_i(A_j\backslash{g})}$. $\aij$ is a quantitative value for how "close" the Allocation $A_i$ is from being (EFX). When we compare all possible comparisons between agents $\astar = min\{ 1,min_{i \neq j}\aij\}$. $\astar = 1$ if and only if, when $A$ is EFX. It is important to note that $\aij$ can be bigger than 1, but $\astar$ is capped at $1$. Furthermore, if $A_j$ has only one good, $\aij$ is not defined. If $\astar = 0,8$, that means that the most neglected agent has $80\%$ of value in his bundle that he needs to be envy-free up to any good. 




\section{Why Round Robin Is EF1, and What EFX Additionally Requires}\label{sec:buffer}
FUNCTION: Restates the known argument that round robin is EF1 and reads it as a buffer that is
never negative. EFX holds exactly when this buffer covers a gap between two single goods. A
worst case example in which the buffer vanishes while the gap stays large shows that round robin
has no constant approximation guarantee for EFX. This motivates the average case question of the
thesis. The buffer and the gap are the explanatory tool for all results in Chapter 4.

\section{Does an EFX Allocation Exist?}\label{sec:existence}
FUNCTION: Summarises where the literature proves EFX existence and defines the region of our
design where it is open. For that region the chapter states explicitly that we assume existence,
and gives the reasons for this assumption. This tells the reader for every result whether a
failure of round robin is a failure of the mechanism or possibly of the problem itself.
